Onboarding, engagement programs, and the first 90 days. Designed for people who do not share a time zone, a language, or an employment model.

The pillar

Employee Lifecycle

Three case studies. Tap any one to open it.

The challenge

High-growth offices were losing new hires early. Onboarding had been written once, in one language, for one way of working, and it did not survive contact with offices that hired differently and faced different market realities.

The research

I spent two months embedded in our largest local office in São Paulo. Working alongside the Local Office Team, I ran more than 40 user research interviews with managers, new employees, and leadership, then reviewed exit interview data across offices to find what repeated from region to region.

What I built

I rebuilt the Global Onboarding Program around 30-60-90-day performance and feedback frameworks. I trained managers to run them and wrote best-practice guides that held up across regions.

The result

Early attrition dropped, and onboarding stopped depending on who your manager happened to be.

The challenge

I had a mandate from the CEO to build a real presence on university campuses and an early-career pipeline. Growth was the stated problem. Scaling the program exposed a deeper one, which was that middle managers had no shared way to manage an intern or connect that work to organizational priorities.

What I built

I redesigned the 10-week program and built pipelines with top universities, owning the candidate journey, recruitment methodology, and visual promotion. Then I wrote the playbook managers run on: an expectations framework covering culture-setting, in-office overlap, project milestones, and final implementation. I prepared interview guidance across three rounds, assessment and case study templates, and an offer workflow with named owners at each step. I created a dedicated Slack channel that turned individual hiring decisions into a shared talent pool, so a strong candidate one team could not place got routed to a team that could.

What I changed after year one

I found that interns on different teams had independently built overlapping projects, because managers had no visibility into what other teams were scoping. I built the fix into the next cycle as a three-step process: managers finalize scope in April, share written briefs in May, and review them as a group before Day 1, with final approval by department heads.

The result

Applications rose 620% and the program delivered the most diverse, highest-performing cohort to date, with a reusable playbook covering the full candidate journey.

The challenge

Endeavor asked me to solve one problem as a hiring exercise. How do you build real connection across 600 people in 42 offices without adding another mandatory thing to anyone’s calendar. I used the asset the organization already had and had never touched, which was its own multilingual workforce.

What I built

I designed four connectivity touchpoints that people climb only if they want to: a 15-minute one-to-one language break, monthly small language groups of four to six, quarterly language exchange lunches of eight to ten, and an annual 600-person Global Staff Language Day. Every rung is gated by a post-session survey, so people advance on their own signal rather than on a schedule. Staying at touchpoint one forever is a valid outcome by design.

How it runs

The engine is a matching algorithm that pairs people on language, CEFR proficiency, hobbies, and availability, then feeds weekly reports to the People team, all done via automation. I grounded the design in research rather than instinct, drawing on second-language learning through social interaction and evidence that language diversity drives creativity at work. I built it to plug into the 30-60-90 onboarding and the Buddy Program rather than compete with them for attention.

The result

The interest in the program made the case for an organization-wide employee language stipend to support proficiency across offices.

Systems & automation.

The infrastructure underneath. Documentation, channel architecture, and the operating model that carries every platform rollout.

The pillar

Systems & Automation

Three case studies. Tap any one to open it.

The challenge

Institutional knowledge lived in people’s heads and scattered across Google Docs, WhatsApp, and errant documentation in 42 offices. New hires and regional teams had nowhere trusted to find best practices, brand assets, or scalable processes. Every answer required finding a person who already knew.

The research

I tested my assumptions vigorously to validate the scale of the problem. I ran user interviews across 42 offices and put early versions of the product in front of staff, so the build addressed the gaps people had actually raised.

What I built

I designed and built the intranet on Notion in a three-month sprint, partnering across Partnerships, Finance, Operations, Platforms, Research, Capital, and Entrepreneur Experience. I worked closely with the Platform team to integrate Notion AI, Salesforce, and Slack so the intranet sat inside our existing stack instead of beside it.

The result

A trusted global source of truth with a complete staff directory, organizational calendar, departmental hubs carrying playbooks and current documentation, and a multimedia learning library, integrated with Salesforce, Slack, HubSpot, and our ATS system. Supported by our Platform Team with automations to remove manual overload and to keep data consistent across channels.

The challenge

We had 73 channels in our Slack directory and more than half were inactive, duplicative, or owned by nobody. Institutional knowledge sank into unmanaged channels and queries went unanswered, which broke down trust in async communication. Senior leadership had stopped showing up because there was no structure worth showing up for, and regional teams had started routing around Slack into WhatsApp entirely.

The research

I ran working sessions with the Platform team and user research interviews with staff and leadership at HQ and Local Offices, which surfaced clear gaps in our communication framework. When one office lead disagreed with removing unused channels, I ran a four-week test period where old and new channels operated in parallel and tracked posting volume, response time, and active membership in both. I let the evidence settle it rather than issuing a decision.

What I built

I created channel governance with standards every surviving channel had to meet: a purpose distinguishable in one sentence and pinned at the top, a named owner accountable for its health, a consistent naming convention across the directory, Slack-native scaffolding, and Salesforce-driven membership so people are added automatically instead of onboarded by hand. Alongside it I co-launched Salesforce-synced profiles using AI workflows, so manager, direct reports, location, division, and start date became visible on every profile.

The result

Channel communication rose 70% in the first 30 days, and every surviving channel had a named owner and a stated purpose.

The challenge

We were protecting the data of 10,000 entrepreneurs and mentors across 42 markets, and almost nobody at the company knew it was happening with our new cybersecurity tool, JumpCloud. The threat landscape was active, with 28,000 attacks blocked in 90 days, a 252% phishing surge, and four affiliate breaches. We needed more offices to opt in and leverage this tool, but the behavior change this needed could not be issued as a directive. Staff were stretched thin, fatigued by competing platform asks, and sensitive to being talked at by headquarters.

The research

I built a focused Cybersecurity Campaign in partnership with senior leadership in our People and Platforms Team, to understand how my role in internal communications and people operations could amplify the tools the Platforms Team was building and to understand where staff were already overloaded. That listening is what produced the audience tiers and the constraints I designed around, rather than a campaign calendar built in isolation.

What I built

I anchored the campaign in one idea staff could hold onto, which is that the same network connecting thousands of entrepreneurs is what makes us a target. I briefed Managing Directors first and gave them WhatsApp-ready comms toolkits, so staff heard it locally before they heard it from HQ. I piloted JumpCloud at headquarters with 70 people before scaling to 600, so friction was found and fixed early. Underneath the campaign I designed the People-Platform Comms Model, which splits every platform initiative into three outputs: comms that translate the work, behavior work that builds the capability the tool requires, and coordination that keeps People close enough to Platform to translate accurately.

The result

Six offices proactively asked to migrate to JumpCloud after a single newsletter outreach, before the campaign formally launched. The People-Platform Comms Model became the template the organization now runs every platform rollout on, including the AI adoption campaign that followed and reached 78% weekly active adoption across 42 offices.

Listening & team.

What I do before I build anything, and what happens when I run a team.

The pillar

Listening & Team

Three case studies. Tap any one to open it.

The challenge

Throughout four U.S. political cycles, our communications function needed to scale 200%, with no team in place and a fully distributed environment to build it in.

What I built

I hired six creatives across social, video, graphic design, editorial, and web development who could work asynchronously, while being mission aligned. To manage the team, I built structured workflows, consistent touchpoints, and real growth paths for each role. I shaped the team using the Entrepreneurial Operating System (EOS), which prioritizes vision, traction, and clarity for lean teams. I owned the budget and the hiring process, and I grew the team’s ownership alongside my own so the function did not bottleneck through me.

The result

A six-person creative team, hired and grown entirely remote, that scaled the communications function 200%. Our work won the 2021 Florida Print Award for Best Use of Color in Design.

The challenge

Our quarterly Staff NPS survey response rate sat below 20%. Leadership could not act on what they could not hear, and staff had stopped believing their answers would result in meaningful change. This was another communication and process that was failing.

What I built

I redesigned the Staff NPS survey and its rollout, sharpening audience segmentation, yearly cadence, and follow-through. Follow-through was the part that mattered most to staff. People wanted to see feedback trends and common threads across offices, so I built reporting that let us navigate anonymity while still surfacing issues that deserved a wider audience.

The result

Responsiveness rose 70% and completion reached 80%, which turned a quiet survey into something leadership used to shape quarterly priorities.

The challenge

Communication between Global HQ and Local Offices had structurally broken down. Global initiatives launched before local offices heard about them, meetings had grown crowded without clear purpose, updates lived scattered across tools with no single source of truth, and local offices stayed siloed from each other across time zones. I could see the function was failing and causing employee engagement to plummet.

The research

I ran a structured audit built on more than 25 user research interviews across Global HQ and local offices, then organized every finding into three tiers: Global-to-Global communication, Global-to-Local communication, and Local-to-Local communication. The tiering mattered because the three groups were experiencing different failures in working together fluidly and a single fix would have served none of them. I paired every problem with a scoped, owned solution rather than leaving an open list of complaints.

The result

The audit surfaced the need for the staff intranet, the Slack restructuring, and the onboarding rebuild, which are three of the projects in this portfolio. It also produced a monthly leadership digest, a quarterly open Q&A for local offices, and the newsletter redesign.
